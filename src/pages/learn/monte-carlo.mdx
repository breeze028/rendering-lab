# Integrals from random samples
<p className="lead">A weighted average can reconstruct an integral.<br/>Its noise tells us where to look next.</p>

<h2 id="estimate">A weighted average</h2>

Let $I=\int_D f(x)\,dx$. Draw independent samples $X_j$ from a normalized density $p$. Then

$$
\widehat I_N=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{f(X_j)}{p(X_j)},\qquad \mathbb E[\widehat I_N]=I.
$$

Division by the PDF compensates for how often a region is visited. Where samples are rare, each visit represents more of the domain. Sampling a direction more often without dividing by its PDF changes the integral instead of improving its estimate.

<h2 id="assumptions">The assumptions matter</h2>

The density must be nonzero wherever the integrand is nonzero, except for sets of zero measure. Finite variance requires $\int f(x)^2/p(x)\,dx<\infty$. Under independent sampling, $\operatorname{Var}[\widehat I_N]=\operatorname{Var}[f(X)/p(X)]/N$. RMS error therefore scales as $N^{-1/2}$ when variance is finite. One particular error curve can rise as more samples arrive; it need not improve monotonically.

A narrow light creates a useful test: uniform samples spend most of their effort on zero contributions. Importance sampling reshapes the density to place more samples where the integrand is large. If $f$ is nonnegative and we could sample $p=f/I$ exactly, every sample would return $I$—but its normalization already requires solving the integral.
